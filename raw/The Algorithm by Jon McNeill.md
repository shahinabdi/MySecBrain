#### Introduction

## What’s in it for me? Discover the winning formula that can help save any troubled business.

What does it take for a business to level up? To not only solve its problems and shortcomings, but turn those fixes into rocket fuel? Well it isn’t necessarily luck, timing, or even talent. The ticket to winning big is actually a repeatable set of five principles that lies at the heart of The Algorithm.

Drawing from his time leading operations at Tesla, Jon McNeill discovered the formula that helped reverse the company’s fortunes during its most fraught, make-or-break period. This formula wasn’t made up of abstract theories. It was forged in the moments where failure was a very real possibility – and speed, clarity, and creativity made all the difference. These insights come from walking the floor, putting yourself in the customer’s shoes, and spotting the opportunities that are often hidden within the problems; it all starts with a willingness to rethink how things are done, no matter how old and trusted a rule or process may be. In this Blink you’ll discover what this winning Algorithm is and how you can make it happen.

#### Chapter 1 of 5

## Question the consensus

It was 2015, when the author, Jon McNeill, was first asked to join the team at the electric vehicle company, Tesla. At first, he was a little confused. Wouldn’t they be better off with someone who had more experience dealing with large-scale car manufacturing? But as Tesla’s co-founder, Elon Musk, explained, that was exactly what he didn’t want.

A big car company exec wouldn’t want to take quick, decisive action; they would look to always maintain the status quo. Tesla had immediate problems, and not a lot of money to fix them, so given McNeill’s years of experience as an entrepreneur helping start-ups grow, he was exactly the kind of guy Musk was looking for. Over the next few years, the author would face a number of challenges, culminating in him learning what is now called the Algorithm – five principles that are all about solving problems and growing your business. Let’s start with the first principle: Question every rule. This one might sound a little obvious coming from the world of start-ups and major disruptors like Tesla or Musk’s other company, SpaceX. But that doesn’t make it any less important.

In fact, it’s pretty fundamental to the Algorithm because it is amazing what you can accomplish when you start taking nothing at face value. Every rule, every requirement, every assumption, now is the time to really second-guess it all. You might be shocked at how many steadfast rules can be broken. As the author discovered early on at Tesla, even laws in foreign countries – countries that aren’t exactly known for their flexibility – can be changed when it’s in everyone’s best interest. In this case, that country was China. Since China was the largest auto market in the world, with deep manufacturing expertise and a strong appetite for electric vehicles, figuring out how to get a foothold in the nation was a major part of the puzzle that needed solving.

The biggest issue was that every foreign company was expected to partner with a national entity. This ultimately meant you’d need to share profits and give up partial ownership. All the global giants that had entered China had agreed to this structure. But Elon Musk wasn’t having it. Even though this was essentially a government law, he didn’t see it as etched in stone. Instead, he directed McNeill and his team to figure out a solution.

So they approached it like a question. What if this rule wasn’t as rigid as it seemed and there was room to negotiate? After all, China had its own priorities; they needed to create jobs and they wanted to be global leaders in clean energy. So the author’s team built a case that aligned Tesla’s goals with China’s ambitions. And sure enough, after about a year of steady negotiation, they reached a breakthrough: approval for Tesla to have the first 100 percent foreign-owned auto plant in China. The advice here isn’t to ignore reality.

These outcomes emerged through the process of examining things more closely and recognizing that many “rules” are shaped by context, incentives, and history. When you can see those elements clearly, you’ll start to see how the rules can change. Many organizations operate within layers of inherited rules. Some are useful, no doubt.

But others can act like handcuffs preventing you from growing and innovating. So look closely at how things work across the business, from product design to internal processes. Ask questions, and see which ones are truly necessary and which ones are holding you back. If someone shrugs their shoulders and says, “I don’t know, that’s how it’s always been done,” that just might be an opportunity for a major improvement.

#### Chapter 2 of 5

## Cleaning up the processes

In this section we’re going to aim for efficiency and combine the second and third parts of the Algorithm. In fact, you might even agree that, in doing so, we’re keeping with the spirit of these two steps. This is because the second one states: “Delete every possible step in a process,” while the third one calls for us to, “Simplify and optimize. ” Let's roll back to 2015 when Tesla was being hit with the perfect storm of problems.

Cash was tight, and timelines were unforgiving; bankruptcy was looming just around the corner. The hunt was, therefore, on to uncover and remove every single inefficiency. Musk had set a bold target to increase online sales twentyfold. That pressure forced the team to look at everything with a sharper eye. This included looking closely at the customer experience. What was slowing down sales?

Where were customers getting stuck? What they discovered was that buying a car on Tesla’s online shop required sixty-four separate clicks. It was a process that culminated in a head-spinning financial document that demanded dozens of signatures. Every step introduced friction and a new opportunity for the customer to check out. So the team zeroed in on a new target: get the whole process down to ten clicks. This meant stripping it all down to its essentials.

Instead of offering customers hundreds of thousands of possible combinations in designing their own car, they narrowed the options into a few well-designed packages. And that financing document? Working with a team at U. S. Bank, Tesla shrank it by 90 percent and created a one-click loan application. Sales jumped quickly as a result.

This wasn’t the only benefit; simplifying everything also had a knock-on effect of making production simpler, tightening up supply chains and reducing costs. So Tesla had great success when they deleted every unnecessary step of the online store process. However, when it came to the third step of the Algorithm, simplifying and optimizing, they noticed a lot of room for improvement in the area of employee training and customer service. The author had been surprised to see there was so much work put into onboarding employees. Tesla wanted its customer service team to be so well-informed that training had ballooned into a month-long process. This was just too long.

Rethinking the process, it became apparent that having employees memorize product details wasn’t going to be what truly impressed customers. So the author got rid of the mountains of training material; he replaced it all with one guiding mantra: “Be so great that they’ll talk about you at dinner. ” It was a mantra that was simple, powerful, and easy to remember. Now, instead of following scripts and checking boxes, staff were reading situations and solving problems in creative ways. Results happened very quickly and positive stories began to circulate. One featured a service manager who stepped in when a family’s car needed a repair during a medical emergency.

He didn’t just bring a temporary replacement vehicle, he went ahead and did a grocery run while the family was at the hospital. That kind of response doesn’t come from a manual. It came from that mantra. You can bet that the family was absolutely talking about Tesla’s customer service over dinner that night. There’s a practical way to approach this in any business. Start by mapping out every step in a process, no matter how small.

Then ask a simple question: which of these steps actually create value for the customer? Those are the ones worth keeping and improving. The rest become candidates for removal, automation, or relocation behind the scenes. More than just efficiency, this creates a cleaner, more focused operation – one that’s easier to scale and more enjoyable for both employees and customers.

#### Chapter 3 of 5

## Closing the cycle gap

At this point, we’ve questioned assumptions, removed unnecessary steps, and simplified the steps that remain. The next step, part four of the Algorithm, is to press on the gas and accelerate cycle time. Now, accelerating cycle time is different than just working faster for the sake of working faster. We’re talking about the kind of speed that is focused on growth – the time it takes to produce, deliver, and learn within the same window of time.

That’s what we mean by cycle time. To really get a grasp on this, and to identify what can be improved, we can differentiate between the two kinds of time within a business: cycle time and touch time. Cycle time is the total time it takes to complete something from start to finish. Touch time is the actual, hands-on work that is being done. In many businesses, the gap between the two is surprisingly large – and that gap is where opportunity lives. One of the best examples to highlight this discrepancy is car repair.

If you’ve ever taken your car into a shop to get something fixed, it might take a week or two before you get it back. That’s the cycle time. But when you finally do get your car back, it’s not unusual to find out that the touch time – the time the mechanic spent actually working on your car, was maybe a couple of hours. By tightening that gap, you can boost efficiency, improve customer experience and unlock far more capacity – all without adding resources. At Tesla, the stakes were even higher. The company’s production cycle lagged far behind industry leaders like Toyota.

That gap came with real financial consequences. The longer it took to build and deliver a car, the longer their money was tied up and unavailable to be reinvested. Shortening the cycle meant quicker revenue turnover, stronger cash flow, and a more resilient business. Oftentimes, this step in The Algorithm boils down to coordination. At Tesla, and later when McNeill was working at the athletic apparel retailer Lululemon, he found that different teams – such as sales, manufacturing, and supply chain – were all operating with their own incomplete, fragmented information. No one could really see the full picture, which meant that plans had to wait for data to arrive or get stitched together based on outdated information.

That made it nearly impossible to move quickly or at scale. At both Tesla and Lululemon, the solution was to open the doors between teams and create a shared, real-time view of the entire operation. With everyone working from the same data, decisions became faster and more accurate. Production could be planned in larger, more efficient batches. Problems like bottlenecks became visible sooner and were fixed more quickly. And as the system improved, cycle times dropped significantly.

In the end, this shows that it is possible to be both quick and smart. It happens when you focus on building cleaner, simpler processes. As you steadily increase the pace, weak points will reveal themselves. Delays, misalignments, and hidden constraints tend to reveal themselves under pressure.

#### Chapter 4 of 5

## Don’t automate what you don’t understand

Now we come to the fifth and final part of the Algorithm, which can be summed up with two simple words: automate last. Automation was a big deal for Elon Musk. One of Tesla’s keystone features was to have its production line be fully automated, with highly advanced robotics, capable of producing cars at scale. In 2017, as they were ramping up to the release of the Model 3, the automated factory even had a very Musk-y name: the Alien Dreadnought.

But the fact was, while these robots were intended to work in perfect harmony, they were rarely cooperative. They jammed, collided, and slowed everything to a crawl. But the real problem wasn’t the robots themselves; the system was too complex. No one really understood the jobs that the robots were supposed to perform. As a result, automation had become the bottleneck rather than the accelerant. Fortunately, the head of automotive product, Jerome Guillen, had the perfect solution.

They’d put up tents and create an emergency production line next to the factory, where they would start building cars by hand until they knew every single step of the assembly process. This allowed the team to spot and fix the inefficiencies that were hiding inside the automated system. Parts were rerouted. Steps were combined or eliminated. Movements could be shortened. By the end, the process was leaner and more intuitive, allowing production to ramp up quickly with far more efficiency than before.

In other words, automate last. This is because automation only works when it’s applied to a process that’s already well understood and tightly optimized. Without that foundation, it’s likely to cause more confusion than anything else. Think of breakthrough companies like Amazon and DoorDash. Before they started automating systems and writing complex software, they learned the business by handling each order manually until they perfected it. This scrappy approach gave them a deep understanding of how the process worked, and where the potential pitfalls lay, which later informed the systems they built at scale.

Only after refining the workflow did they begin to automate. There’s a natural temptation to jump straight to automation. It feels fast, modern, and efficient. But when the underlying process is still messy or unclear, automation can lock in those flaws and make them harder to fix. The better approach is to treat automation as the final layer. First, map the process.

Then simplify it. Then optimize it through repetition and refinement. Once the flow is clear and stable, automation can step in and multiply the gains. It’s the amplifier. And when it’s applied at the right moment, it can turn a well-designed process into something truly powerful.

#### Chapter 5 of 5

## Maintaining urgency and being the customer

Up to this point, we’ve been focusing on how the Algorithm can fix your business operations. But for it to really take hold, it helps when the business has three cultural elements in place. In this final section we’re going to quickly run through these important ingredients that can easily go missing. First, we’re going to look at issues of urgency and accountability.

At Tesla, urgency wasn’t left to chance. It was built into the rhythm of the company, driven directly by leadership. Say what you want about Elon Musk, but he knows his products inside and out, and his attention is always locked on improving them, down to the smallest details. Each week, a strategic meeting was held, addressing the most important problems. Small teams were assigned, goals were set, and they knew exactly what was coming next: accountability in the form of a direct conversation with Musk next Tuesday. Because the CEO understood the fine details – they couldn’t bullshit their way through the meeting.

They had to be sharp, prepared, and honest about what was and wasn’t working. They had to come prepared with progress, data, and real answers. That dynamic created a powerful kind of urgency and accountability, and it all hinges on two things: a knowledgeable, well-informed CEO, and routine, weekly deadlines where nobody expects an A for effort – the expectation is always progress. Now, the final two cultural ingredients go hand-in-hand with one another. They’re both about the customer. The first is to make sure you consider the entire customer experience when considering your product.

And the second is to eat your own dog food – which is just a memorable way of saying, use your own product. So, for example, Tesla could produce a car that is fun and comfortable to drive, but that’s not the entire experience. If the other parts – be it charging, setup, or maintenance – become an inconvenient hassle, the overall experience is going to be tainted. On the plus side, discovering these friction points can be an opportunity. For instance, car insurance can be one such hassle. So Tesla started bundling insurance as part of the product, which turned it into a nice supplemental income stream.

But the best way to discover any issue is to be a customer and get to know every single part of the experience yourself. At Lululemon, executives test prototypes themselves. They’ll go out jogging in the latest running gear and come back with valuable firsthand knowledge. You won’t get a quicker feedback loop than that. At Tesla, McNeill formalized this idea with what he called the “20 percent rule. ” One day each week was spent experiencing different parts of the customer journey – everything from driving the car to interacting with service or delivery.

It was all about gaining valuable insights and understanding what needed attention. And that work never really ends. What you improve today will become the baseline tomorrow. New expectations and competitors will emerge and the cycle continues.

The real advantage comes from staying in that loop – constantly testing, learning, and adjusting. When you combine that mindset with a deep, personal understanding of the customer experience, you move beyond just reacting. You’re in control, shaping your own future.

#### Conclusion

## Final summary

The main takeaway of this Blink to The Algorithm by Jon McNeill is that the Algorithm is a five-step process that helps any business to strip away inefficiencies and improve what truly matters so that you can achieve speed, clarity, and growth. Step one is to question every rule. Step two is to delete every possible step in a process. Step three is to simplify and optimize the remaining steps.

Step four is to accelerate cycle time. And step five is to automate last, after you have already mastered the process. The Algorithm is a way of breaking everything into its smallest parts, studying each one closely, and asking why it exists at all. That level of scrutiny exposes hidden inefficiencies and opens the door to better, often surprising solutions. Leaders stay close to the work, creating urgency through frequent, focused problem-solving and holding teams accountable for real progress. At the same time, they widen their lens, looking beyond the product itself to the entire customer experience – where frustration can also signal opportunity.

And they stay grounded by using their own products, uncovering issues firsthand and accelerating feedback loops. When these elements come together, improvement becomes continuous. Thanks so much for listening. See you in the next Blink.
